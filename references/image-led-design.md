# Image-led and mixed-media design

Use this reference when a carousel should feel observed, lived-in, poetic, photographic, illustrative, or materially human rather than like a stack of knowledge cards.

## Begin with perception

Before choosing components, write five short fields:

1. **feeling** — what should the reader feel before reading?
2. **scene** — what place, object, gesture, or moment can embody the idea?
3. **visual anchor** — what should the eye find first?
4. **human trace** — what imperfection, handwriting, crop, annotation, or material mark keeps the page from feeling synthetic?
5. **memory trigger** — what visual detail might remain after the wording is forgotten?

These fields belong in the visible design brief, not hidden reasoning.

## Choose an image-text relationship

Every image must do at least one job:

- `embody` — the image is the claim in visual form;
- `evidence` — the image proves or documents a claim;
- `annotate` — text names details inside the image;
- `contrast` — image and text deliberately disagree or reveal a gap;
- `echo` — a motif returns with changed meaning;
- `transition` — a low-text image gives the reader an emotional pause.

Do not add an image whose only role is “make it prettier.”

## Visual modes

- `editorial`: typography and evidence remain primary; images are rare and documentary.
- `mixed`: structure pages alternate with image-led pages.
- `image-led`: most pages begin with a visual anchor and use short explanatory copy.
- `photo-diary`: photographs, translucent layers, dates, field notes, tape, and observed detail.
- `object-study`: one object or symbol is examined from page to page.
- `poetic-poster`: sparse language, large negative space, one emotional image, and a restrained signal color.

The mode is a content decision, not a permanent house style.

## Asset plan

For each planned asset record:

- source: user-provided, original illustration, generated, licensed, or public domain;
- semantic role: hero, evidence, atmosphere, texture, motif, or transition;
- focal point and intended crop;
- treatment: plain, paper, bleed, cutout, or monochrome;
- image-text relationship;
- provenance note when publication requires attribution.

Never reuse a benchmark creator's published image, watermark, signature, avatar, characteristic stamp, or distinctive identity treatment.

## Material restraint

Materiality helps only when it feels coherent. Select two or three from:

- paper fiber or photocopy grain;
- pencil, ink, or crayon line;
- restrained tape or translucent paper;
- halftone, misregistration, or one-color print;
- soft physical shadow;
- handwritten observation notes.

Do not use every treatment in one deck. Avoid adding film grain, torn paper, tape, stamps, and doodles merely as a style checklist.

## Cadence

For a 6–10 page mixed deck, a useful starting rhythm is:

1. image-led promise;
2. structural distinction;
3. visual mechanism or near-wordless pause;
4. documentary evidence;
5. image-text contrast;
6. decision or limitation;
7. human judgment;
8. visual resolution and action.

Change the rhythm when the content demands it. The rule is perceptual variety, not a fixed page count.

## Review questions

- If the explanatory text is covered, does the image still carry a feeling or idea?
- Is the visual anchor visible within one second at phone size?
- Is the crop intentional, especially around faces, hands, and objects?
- Does text point to, answer, or transform the image instead of repeating it?
- Is there at least one human trace without pretending to be handmade?
- Are consecutive pages visually distinct for a semantic reason?
- Would this still look meaningful without generic “AI” symbols, glowing interfaces, robots, or neon?
